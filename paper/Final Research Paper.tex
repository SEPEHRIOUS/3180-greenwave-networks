\begin{abstract}
The development of sixth generation (6G) wireless networks is expected to change how future communication systems operate by making artificial intelligence (AI) a core part of the network. Unlike previous generations, AI-native 6G networks can use AI to improve network performance, automatically manage resources, and support applications such as smart cities, autonomous vehicles, and mobile healthcare. However, these improvements also raise concerns about privacy, security, consumer trust, and the ethical use of AI. This paper introduces AI-native 6G networks by explaining why AI is becoming an important part of future wireless systems and describing the technologies that support intelligent network operations. It also provides the background necessary to understand the privacy and ethical issues discussed later in the paper.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Wireless communication has changed significantly over the past few decades as technology continues to improve. Every new generation of wireless networks has introduced better performance and new features \cite{chen2023}. While 4G focused on a faster mobile internet, 5G brought improvements such as lower latency, higher speeds, and support for a much larger number of connected devices \cite{lv2025}. As new technologies continue to develop, including smart cities, autonomous transportation, industrial automation, and mobile healthcare, future wireless networks will need to meet even greater demands for speed, reliability, and intelligent decision-making \cite{wang2026}.

The next generation of wireless communication, known as 6G, is expected to build on these improvements by making artificial intelligence (AI) a built-in part of the network instead of simply using it as an external tool \cite{janbi2020}. AI-native 6G networks will be able to monitor network conditions, predict traffic, allocate resources more efficiently, and make decisions automatically with very little human involvement \cite{nurakhov2026}. By combining AI techniques with advanced communication technologies, these networks are expected to become more flexible, efficient, and capable of supporting billions of connected devices \cite{dogra2021}.

Using AI throughout the network offers several advantages. AI can help automate network management, improve resource allocation, reduce delays, and increase overall network performance \cite{porambage2021}. It can also help networks respond quickly to changing conditions by making adjustments in real time \cite{taghia2024}. These improvements will become increasingly important as more devices generate large amounts of data and require reliable communication \cite{ju2024}.

Although AI-native 6G offers many benefits, it also creates new challenges. Large amounts of data will be collected and processed, raising concerns about user privacy, data ownership, and cyber-security \cite{chabira2025}. AI systems may also make decisions that are difficult to explain, creating questions about fairness, transparency, and accountability. Due to these concerns, understanding the foundations of AI-native 6G is important before discussing the privacy, ethical, and consumer trust issues that come with future wireless networks.

\section{Background}
Wireless communication has continued to evolve with each new generation of mobile networks. While 5G introduced faster communication, lower latency, and support for more connected devices, 6G is expected to expand these capabilities even further. Future 6G networks are expected to provide higher data rates, lower latency, improved reliability, and support for massive numbers of connected devices. They will also combine communication and sensing technologies, allowing wireless signals to both transmit information and collect data about the surrounding environment. These features will support applications such as smart healthcare, intelligent transportation, and smart city infrastructure.

One of the biggest differences between 5G and 6G is the role of artificial intelligence. In AI-native 6G, AI is built directly into the network instead of being added afterward. This allows the network to learn from collected data, adjust to changing conditions, and improve its performance automatically. AI can help manage network traffic, allocate resources, improve energy efficiency, and increase network reliability. For example, AI can predict when network traffic will increase and automatically assign more resources before congestion becomes a problem.

Several AI technologies make this possible. Machine learning can analyze network data and predict future traffic patterns. Deep learning improves tasks such as signal processing and channel estimation. Reinforcement learning allows networks to learn from experience and make better decisions over time. Federated learning also helps improve privacy by allowing devices to train AI models locally without sending personal data to a central server.

Even with these advantages, AI-native 6G still faces several challenges. The large amount of data collected by future networks raises concerns about privacy and user control. AI decision-making can also create problems related to transparency, fairness, and accountability. In addition, protecting AI models from cyberattacks and ensuring reliable network performance remain important research areas. Solving these challenges will be necessary to build secure, trustworthy, and responsible AI-native 6G networks.

\section{Literature Review: Privacy Risks in 6G Networks}
Privacy in 6G networks has become one of the most heavily researched areas as the
network architecture moves toward AI-native operation. Porambage et
al.~\cite{porambage2021} survey how security and privacy will impact envisioned 6G
systems, examining the challenges introduced by different 6G technologies and the
potential solutions across areas such as AI and machine-learning security,
physical-layer security, and privacy preservation. Nguyen et al.~\cite{nguyen2021}
complement this roadmap with a systematic overview organized by network layer,
covering the physical, connection, and service layers. Two lessons from their survey
are especially relevant: 6G does not merely inherit vulnerabilities from previous
generations, and its new radio technologies introduce entirely new threat vectors,
such as the exposed location of radio stripes in ultra-massive MIMO systems at
Terahertz bands and attacks against the network's pervasive intelligence itself.
Together, these surveys frame 6G privacy not as a single problem but as a set of
challenges distributed across the technologies and layers that make up the network,
each requiring its own class of solution.

A key theme across this literature is that the architecture enabling AI-native
operation also expands the attack surface. As distributed artificial intelligence is
embedded throughout the network to support services such as smart environments and
the Internet of Everything~\cite{janbi2020}, more points in the system collect,
process, and exchange data, increasing the number of places where privacy can be
compromised. This concern is sharpest at the network edge: Mao et
al.~\cite{mao2023} survey security and privacy on the 6G network edge specifically,
where computation and data processing move closer to users and outside traditionally
hardened core infrastructure. Sandeepa et al.~\cite{sandeepa2022} similarly identify
new privacy challenges specific to B5G/6G and map out open research directions,
reinforcing that many of these challenges remain unsolved rather than merely
unimplemented.

This tension is especially visible in federated learning, which is often presented
as a privacy-preserving technique. Federated learning improves privacy by allowing
devices to train AI models locally and share only model updates instead of raw
data~\cite{taghia2024}. However, this same mechanism introduces new challenges
around the reliability and security of those shared updates in a self-regulated 6G
environment, meaning the technique reduces one form of privacy risk while
introducing new questions about how the learning process itself can be trusted and
secured. Overall, the literature suggests that AI in 6G must be treated as both a
means of protecting privacy and a new source of privacy risk.

\section{Discussion}

The privacy literature and the consumer trust literature approach the same underlying
problem from opposite directions, and comparing them exposes a gap that neither fully
addresses. The privacy-focused work, such as Porambage et al.'s
roadmap~\cite{porambage2021} and Nguyen et al.'s layered survey~\cite{nguyen2021}, is
strongest at locating where risks technically originate across 6G technologies, but it
tends to treat the user as a subject to be protected rather than an active participant.
The trust-focused work reverses this emphasis: it centers user perception, skepticism,
and acceptance, but rarely connects those concerns back to specific technical
mechanisms. Work on trust anchor technologies~\cite{veith2023} begins to bridge this
divide by asking which technical components a trustworthy 6G network can be anchored
on, but it remains focused on infrastructure rather than user-facing control. What is
still missing is a mechanism through which users can exercise meaningful, granular
control over the specific AI-driven functions processing their data. This gap directly
motivates the proposed approach discussed in the following section.

\section{Conclusion}

From the privacy perspective specifically, this survey shows that risk in AI-native 6G
is layered rather than monolithic, and that the AI techniques often promoted as privacy
solutions, such as federated learning, simultaneously introduce new attack surfaces of
their own. Future research should prioritize governance mechanisms that operate at the
same level as the AI functions themselves, rather than relying solely on
application-layer regulation designed for an earlier generation of networks.
