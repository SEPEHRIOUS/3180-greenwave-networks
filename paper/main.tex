\section{Literature Review: Privacy Risks in 6G Networks}
Privacy in 6G networks has become one of the most heavily researched areas as the
network architecture moves toward AI-native operation. Nguyen et al.~\cite{nguyen2021}
conducted a comprehensive review of emerging technologies for secure communication in
6G networks and developed a hierarchical taxonomy of security challenges spanning
physical-layer attacks, network-level threats, device vulnerabilities, and
application-specific risks. This layered framing shows that privacy risk in 6G is not a
single problem but originates at different levels of the network, each requiring
different mitigations.

A systematic review synthesizing peer-reviewed studies on privacy preservation in 6G
networks~\cite{personaldata2026} connects technical privacy-enhancing technologies to
regulatory compliance frameworks such as GDPR. This links the engineering side of
privacy to the governance side, where cross-jurisdiction regulatory fragmentation
complicates data governance for multi-domain 6G systems.

A recurring theme in this literature is that AI itself introduces new privacy risks
rather than only solving them. Techniques promoted as privacy-preserving, such as
federated learning, keep raw data on the device but remain vulnerable to attacks such as
model inversion, in which shared model updates are reverse-engineered to infer
information about the original data. This demonstrates a central tension in our survey:
the same AI-native features marketed as privacy-preserving can simultaneously open new
privacy attack surfaces.

\section{Discussion}
% TODO (Manraj): paste Manraj's discussion paragraph.

The privacy literature and the consumer trust literature approach the same underlying
problem from opposite directions, and comparing them exposes a gap neither fully
addresses. The privacy-focused work, such as Nguyen et al.'s layered taxonomy, is
strongest at locating where risks technically originate, but treats the user largely as
a passive subject to be protected. The trust-focused work reverses this: it centers user
perception, skepticism, and acceptance, but rarely connects those concerns back to
specific technical mechanisms. What is missing in both bodies of literature is a bridge
between them: a mechanism through which users can exercise meaningful, granular control
over the specific AI-driven functions processing their data. Regulatory frameworks such
as GDPR partially fill this role at the application level, but regulatory fragmentation
across jurisdictions leaves network-level AI functions in a governance gray zone. This
gap directly motivates the proposed approach in the following section.

\section{Conclusion}
% TODO (Manraj): paste Manraj's conclusion paragraph.

From the privacy perspective specifically, this survey shows that risk in AI-native 6G
is layered rather than monolithic, and that the AI techniques often promoted as privacy
solutions, such as federated learning, simultaneously introduce new attack surfaces of
their own. Future research should prioritize governance mechanisms that operate at the
same level as the AI functions themselves, rather than relying solely on application-layer
regulation designed for an earlier generation of networks.
